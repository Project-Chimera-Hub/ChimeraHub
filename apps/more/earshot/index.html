Headphone check

Two short tests. The first makes sure left and right are the right way round. The second shows how well you can hear direction with these headphones.

1. Left or right

Play a sound, then choose the side it came from. Four rounds.

2. Point to the sound

Each sound comes from one direction at ear level. Click where you heard it on the circle, or turn the pointer with ← and → and press Enter. Eight rounds.

About Earshot

Earshot turns multiple object tracking, a classic attention task usually done with the eyes, into a listening task. It follows the structure of 3D multiple object tracking (3D-MOT) and rebuilds each step for hearing.

How a trial works

  1. Listen. Every sound clicks from where it starts.
  2. Targets. A bell rings from each target while the others stay quiet.
  3. Remember them. All the sounds click again for a second, now identical.
  4. Track. The sounds move for a few seconds, bouncing off each other and off the edges of the arena.
  5. Choose. Everything stops. Listen to each sound where it ended up, pick the targets and confirm. Bells then ring from where the targets really were.

How the score works

After a correct trial the next one is faster. After a mistake it is slower. Each step changes the speed by about 26% at first, then by about 12% once the speed starts going back and forth. Your speed threshold is the average of those turning points: the speed you get right about half the time.

A threshold only compares fairly with other sessions that use the same arena, number of sounds, number of targets and tracking time. Each new session starts a little below your last threshold for that setup.

Why the sounds are built this way

In the visual task every ball looks the same once the targets are marked, so the only way to follow a target is to keep track of where it is. Earshot keeps that rule for sound.

  • All sounds use the same recipe, and every click is a fresh burst of noise, so no sound has its own timbre.
  • The sounds take turns in a newly shuffled order each round, so none of them owns a rhythm.
  • Loudness varies slightly from click to click, so loudness can’t give a sound away. On the floor arena distance still changes loudness, just as it would in a room.
  • Short, broadband clicks are the default because they are the easiest sounds to locate.

Getting good results

  • Use headphones, not speakers. Turn off any spatial audio or head tracking feature on your device, because Earshot already places the sounds.
  • Keep your head still and face the screen.
  • Run the headphone check once. If front and back get mixed up, use the Front only option.
  • Expect the first sessions to feel hard. Hearing direction improves with practice.
  • When a setup gets easy, add a sound or a target rather than chasing more speed.

With spoken prompts turned on in the settings, Earshot reads out trial numbers, sound numbers and results, so it can be played without looking at the screen.

Limits

Earshot places sounds with your browser’s built-in, generic head-related transfer function (HRTF). That works well for left and right, less well for front and back, and poorly for height, and it varies between people and headphones. The Floor and Dome arenas are experimental.

Earshot is a prototype for exploring spatial listening and attention. It isn’t a medical or diagnostic tool and makes no claim to improve thinking, sport performance or anything else. It is an independent project, not affiliated with NeuroTracker or CogniSens.
