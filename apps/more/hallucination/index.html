Controlled Hallucination

Controlled hallucination, learned from the bottom up: first the images your visual system already makes without being asked, then taking them over, then making your own.

How it works

Seeing is already a hallucination, just one kept on a short lead. What you see is the brain's running best guess about what is out there, built top-down from expectation and memory, and corrected bottom-up by the signal from the eyes wherever the guess and the signal disagree. Most of the time the signal wins, and the guess is so good that it feels like the world itself.

To steer what you see, the bottom-up stream has to be loosened so that the top-down stream has room. Each exercise below loosens it in a different way:

tiring it out — an adapted eye reports what is no longer there (afterimages), or stops reporting what still is (fading);
leaving it undecided — a faint object at the edge of vision that comes and goes, an image with two readings, or pure noise, where the input cannot settle the question and you can;
cutting it short — a flash, gone before the image is finished, so you finish it;
taking it away — a featureless field, or only words.

They are ordered by how much the screen still gives you, from everything to nothing. Each one trains two things together: induction, getting the visual system to produce something from little or nothing, and control, steering what it produces with attention, expectation and imagery. Read each one's notes before trying it: knowing what is happening, and why, is most of the skill.
